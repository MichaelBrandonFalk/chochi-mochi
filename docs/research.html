Chochi Mochi · Notes for grown-ups

Curriculum sources and adaptation notes

Where the ideas come from, how the game uses them, and what the game can and cannot teach.

Researched November 2, 2026. The official program and author overview were rechecked November 12, 2026.

This game is an original educational adaptation. It does not reproduce a SPACE manual or treatment protocol, and it is not an endorsed SPACE product. The game has not received a clinical review or clinical efficacy testing.

Primary and authoritative sources

  1. Official SPACE program, developed by Eli Lebowitz. Establishes the parent-based approach and the dual focus on supportive responses and reducing accommodation.
  2. SPACE overview by Eli Lebowitz, International OCD Foundation. Describes validation plus confidence, mapping accommodation, selecting and announcing adult changes, and trained professional delivery.
  3. Lebowitz et al., 2019 randomized noninferiority study. Randomized 124 children ages 7–14 to parent-based SPACE or child CBT; 97 completed all sessions and assessments. SPACE was noninferior on the studied anxiety outcomes; accommodation decreased more in SPACE. This trial does not establish efficacy of this game, universal suitability, or equivalence for every family.

Lesson map

The 19 lessons are small teaching units chosen for the story, not official SPACE modules or session counts. All are original paraphrases and fictional examples. Broad teaching principles derive from sources 1–2; nature metaphors, listening exercises, and keepsakes are our educational presentation.

Adaptation boundaries

  • Children can learn kindness but are not responsible for treating a peer or parent. Glaloula need not become anxiety-free to finish the story.
  • The game is not exposure therapy and assigns no real-world accommodation-reduction tasks to children. Caregivers lead real treatment plans.
  • Keep appropriate medical, disability, sensory, developmental, and safety supports. Not all assistance is anxiety accommodation.
  • Individual plans, pace, and difficult reactions need clinical judgment. A caregiver’s warm consistency is not permission for coercion or ignoring needs.
  • Simple prose, short paragraphs, and concrete fantasy examples target roughly age seven. No formal reading-age certification is claimed. Adult notes are intentionally more advanced.
  • The five-part final gathering applies the 19 ideas through new fictional situations. It is not a standardized or clinical exam.
  • No copied artwork, logos, questionnaires, proprietary manual text, or treatment worksheets are included.

Adaptation history

These entries record earlier editions. They describe how the educational story grew; they do not add treatment instructions.

Expanded story edition (1.1.0)

Each place now includes arrival, an optional discovery, a worked teaching scene, guided rehearsal, and a knowledge choice with a closing scene. The three-page prologue introduces Glaloula as a fairy and Aunt Fern as her caregiver. These fictional examples explain the existing principles; they do not add a prescribed treatment plan.

Practice edition (1.2.0)

Optional card activities rehearse the existing concepts without real-world assignments. The final gathering uses new fictional situations to review each of the 19 principles. Practice completion measures only game interaction, never mental health.

Responsive world edition (1.3.0)

Discovery light and garden flowers respond to reading and game choices, not mental-health measures. The four final-review story scenes preserve the distinction between friendship and caregiver-led treatment. No additional treatment techniques or individual care prescriptions are introduced.

Learning and character edition (1.4.0)

New fictional teacher memories and Glaloula’s letters connect the principles to character goals. Conversation branches show differences between well-intended responses without claiming that a particular sentence reliably reduces distress. Teach-back and new-situation exercises rehearse conceptual understanding; they are not clinical assessments. Own-words practice records no audio or personal text. No real-world treatment tasks are assigned.

Version 2 game design

The garden art afternoon, creative forks, hexagon route, craft projects, and five-part finale are original game design, not clinical SPACE session activities. All 19 ideas remain represented in the finale. The fictional caregiver Fern leads planning with a trained helper; Chochi and Mochi practice friendship. Needed access and care remain in place. Progress and decorative brightness mark learning and story completion, not Glaloula’s anxiety or treatment outcome. Creative choices change fictional details and never ask for a player’s own mental-health history.

Curriculum expansion (3.4.0)

The parent-only path, Learn with Aunt Fern, is available before the child prologue and independently of all story completion. Nineteen original notes follow one fictional art-time example through support, observation, target selection, a detailed adult plan, advance announcement, implementation, preparation for reactions, and review. These are topics, not nineteen clinical sessions. Sources 1–2 support the parent-focused sequence, acceptance plus confidence, detailed plans, and preparation for difficult reactions. Source 3 supports the treatment background, not the game’s efficacy.

The child story adds a four-part accommodation loop, paired access-versus-taking-over examples, and a precise three-part plan. New-story practice revisits ideas at levels 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, 14, 16, and 19. The finale retains all nineteen ideas and five acts; its seven-card planning activity now unfolds in three small groups. A response need not produce agreement or calm to be supportive. Access and genuine new needs still require attention.

The early-reader explanations, low table and ribbon examples, Fern’s plan, review prompts, and practice alternatives are original educational design. They are not copied or validated SPACE instruments. No family information is requested or recorded by the parent notes or mixed practice. Read-aloud uses the existing browser speech option. A SPACE-trained licensed clinician should review any proposed clinical use; this implementation has not received that independent review.

Applied learning edition (3.5.0)

Each parent note now replaces its passive reflection block with one fictional decision and explanatory feedback for both options. Note 19 shows three original observations from Fern’s book, including a missed planned response. These are not copied clinical worksheets, a treatment schedule, or a score for Glaloula. No personal answers are requested or saved.

All nineteen previously unused transfer stories are available inside the existing Remember page’s optional new-story section. Earlier mixed-practice cases remain reachable one at a time using Another little story and Previous little story. Changing the story preserves the session’s feedback, focuses the revealed heading, and stops current narration. Practice does not gate gifts, letters, or chapters.

Examples now include repeated picnic-bag checking and family routine changes, alongside the connected paint-choice story. They invite examination of function, context, access, needs, and adult actions rather than automatic withdrawal of help. Preparation for difficult moments and adult support is introduced during planning, before announcement and implementation. The five-act finale uses specific adult decisions and ends with an honest review of all three fictional observations. Existing card targets and save formats remain stable.

The official program and the author’s IOCDF overview support the parent-focused principles and preparation for reactions. The stories, interactions, feedback, and diary are our educational design, not a validated implementation of the treatment. Independent clinical review remains outstanding.
